The Long Pour notes on making coffee slowly, at home
on brewing

Four minutes is longer than it sounds

A kettle, a filter, and roughly the time it takes water to fall through a bed of ground coffee — that's the entire method. It has survived a century of faster alternatives for reasons that have very little to do with speed.

There are faster ways to make coffee. A machine will do it in under a minute, no attention required, no risk of getting it wrong. Pour-over asks for the opposite of that: a kettle you have to hold, water poured in slow, deliberate circles, four minutes where the only job is to watch and wait. It is, by most reasonable measures, an inefficient way to make a cup of coffee.

And yet it hasn't gone anywhere. Part of the reason is the cup itself — hand-poured coffee tends to taste cleaner, brighter, less muddled than what a machine produces, because you can adjust for the bean in front of you instead of running everything through the same fixed cycle. But a lot of people who do this daily will admit, if you ask, that the taste isn't really what keeps them doing it. It's the four minutes.

Where the method comes from

The idea is older than most people assume, and the timeline says something about why it stuck around:

  • 1908

    Melitta Bentz, a German housewife, punches holes in a brass pot and lines it with blotting paper — the first paper filter, built to keep grounds out of the cup without the bitterness of boiling.

  • 1950s–60s

    Filter coffee becomes a household staple across Europe and North America, mostly through electric drip machines that automate the pour rather than replace it.

  • 1990s

    Japanese brewers, particularly at Kono and later Hario, start refining manual pour-over as a precise, repeatable craft rather than a chore — measured water, measured time, a specific kettle shape.

  • 2000s onward

    Third-wave coffee shops bring manual brewing back to the counter, this time as something to watch being made, not just something to order.

The machine makes coffee while you do something else. The kettle makes coffee while you do nothing else — and that turns out to be the appeal, not the cost.

What four minutes actually involves

Every method has its own numbers, but a fairly standard pour looks something like this, start to finish:

0:00

Pour just enough hot water to wet all the grounds — roughly twice their weight. Wait. This is the bloom: trapped gas escapes, and the coffee should visibly swell.

0:45

Begin the first real pour, working in slow circles from the centre outward, keeping the water level steady rather than flooding it.

1:30

Pause briefly, let the level drop a little, then pour again — most methods use two or three staged pours rather than one continuous stream.

2:45

Final pour reaches the target water weight. Bed should look roughly flat, not cratered on one side — that's usually a sign of uneven pouring.

3:30–4:00

Drawdown finishes as the last water filters through. Total brew time in this range is typical for a light-to-medium roast.

Where it usually goes wrong

Most disappointing cups trace back to one of three things: a grind that's too fine, which chokes the flow and pulls out too much bitterness; water that's too hot, scorching the grounds rather than dissolving them evenly; or a pour that's too fast, which floods the bed and drains before the flavour has had time to extract properly. None of this needs expensive equipment to fix — mostly it needs one variable changed at a time, and a willingness to make a few bad cups on the way to a good one.

A flat, even bed of grounds after drawdown usually means the pour was even too.

  • ·Weigh the water and the coffee rather than guessing — consistency depends on it more than any other single habit.
  • ·Grind just before brewing. Pre-ground coffee loses most of its aroma within days.
  • ·Keep the pour slow and centred; resist the urge to fill the dripper quickly.
  • ·Taste before adjusting. A sour cup usually needs a finer grind or more time; a bitter one usually needs the opposite.

Four minutes, on purpose

Nobody needs pour-over coffee. A machine, or even instant, gets caffeine into a cup with far less effort. What the kettle offers instead is a small, unhurried stretch of time that asks for nothing but attention — which may be the real reason it has outlasted almost every appliance built to replace it.

If you're trying it for the first time, don't aim for a perfect cup. Aim for four unhurried minutes. The coffee tends to follow.
